Focus troubleshooting

Why Your Climbing Video Goes Out of Focus

Separate focus hunting from motion blur and upload compression. Test camera placement, focus controls, and saved originals before recording another attempt.

Published November 8, 2026 · By the CruxCut team

Find where the softness starts

A climbing clip can look sharp before the attempt, soften as someone walks past the camera, then become sharp again. That is a different problem from detail disappearing only after an upload. Start with the unchanged recording, not a social-media preview, and find the first moment when the climber becomes hard to read.

This guide is a practical capture test. It does not promise that an editor can restore detail that was never recorded, or that locking focus is right for every wall. CruxCut is not presented as a focus-repair tool.

Distinguish three kinds of blur

  • Focus changes: a stationary hold or wall texture becomes soft while something nearer the camera becomes sharp. Repeated soft-to-sharp transitions may suggest the camera is refocusing, but the appearance alone does not prove the cause.
  • Motion blur: stationary wall detail stays clearer while a moving hand or foot smears. A focus lock will not necessarily fix this.
  • Sharing-quality loss: the original looks readable but the downloaded or posted copy loses detail. Use the separate sharing-quality checklist rather than changing capture settings based only on a compressed preview.

Pause at the same timestamp in the original and saved edit. Then play both at normal speed. Paused frames help locate a change; normal playback shows whether it interferes with reading the movement. If only one player looks soft, compare another available player before concluding the source is damaged.

Check the simple causes first

  1. Inspect the camera lens for chalk dust, fingerprints, condensation, or an obstructing case. Clean it gently using the device maker's guidance, not a chalky shirt.
  2. Keep fingers, straps, bags, and loose clothing away from the lens. A phone resting too close behind an object may frame it prominently.
  3. Choose a safe viewpoint with fewer people crossing immediately in front of the camera. Never put the phone in a landing zone or block a walkway to obtain a clearer image.
  4. Check the actual recording lens and zoom you selected. A highly enlarged preview is not evidence that the captured movement contains more detail.

If the whole image remains hazy throughout, a repeated autofocus adjustment may not be the main issue. Do not keep changing focus settings to compensate for a dirty lens or light reflecting through a nearby object.

Make a short automatic-focus baseline

Before climbing, record a short clip from the intended camera position. Include textured holds around the climbing area and a person moving through the expected space, with their permission. Review the saved clip, not just the live preview. Note whether the wall, climber, or a nearby passerby becomes the sharpest part.

Keep the phone position, recording app, lens, resolution, and frame rate unchanged for a second test. Change only the focus control you are investigating. If several settings change together, you cannot tell which change helped.

Try focus control only if your recording mode supports it

Check the instructions for your exact device and camera app. A still-photo setting is not proof that the same control works during video. Apple documents touch-and-hold AE/AF Lock in its camera-tools guide, while its Camera Control guide describes a separate supported-model method that can lock focus and exposure for photos and video.

The Camera Control method is not a hands-free filming recipe: Apple says to keep a finger touching the control to maintain the lock, and releasing it turns the lock off. Do not assume it stays locked after placing the phone down. Look for an actually supported recording-mode control and verify its behavior in a saved test. If no suitable control exists, keep automatic focus and improve the viewpoint instead.

Where a recording-mode focus lock is available, choose a subject or textured area at the distance where the climbing movement will occur, rather than an object beside the phone. Check the near and far parts of the attempt. A fixed focus distance can help one part of the scene while leaving another soft, especially if the climber moves substantially toward or away from the camera.

Exposure and focus may be linked

AE/AF means automatic exposure and automatic focus. A combined lock can affect brightness as well as focus. After making a change, inspect light and dark parts of the wall and the climber's hands. A sharper-looking area is not a useful improvement if important holds disappear into darkness or bright highlights.

Do not copy a single exposure value from another gym. Lighting, wall color, camera position, and available controls differ. If the problem is repeated brightness pulses or moving dark bands rather than softness, follow the separate indoor-light flicker test.

Keep the camera position useful for the full move

A closer view is not always easier to film. Check that the camera can see the full movement without relying on a very near foreground object as a focus target. Follow the filming-position checklist and keep hands, feet, and the expected finish inside the frame.

Test the same viewpoint with the start and finish positions represented, where safe and practical. Do not climb or stand in someone else's fall zone to test a camera. If getting a test person into position is unsafe, use visible wall texture to check the scene and accept that the moving-climber result still needs verification.

Decide from the saved result, not the setting name

  • Compare the automatic baseline and the single-change test at the same parts of the movement.
  • Check whether a foreground crossing still causes a visible change.
  • Check both the closest and farthest climbing positions, not only the starting hold.
  • Keep the setting only if the saved footage remains readable throughout. Return to the baseline if the fixed distance makes later movement worse.

If the source is already soft, keep the untouched original. A crop can make the subject larger without restoring missing detail. Work on a copy if you try sharpening in another tool, and inspect hands and hold edges for artificial halos rather than treating a stronger effect as recovered information.

Before asking for help

Save two short labeled originals with one controlled difference. Record the phone model, camera app, recording mode, lens or zoom, and the focus control you actually used. Say whether the problem exists in the original, editor preview, saved output, or posted version. Share only footage you have permission to share and avoid private conversations or identifiable bystanders without consent.

Once capture is readable, use the vertical-crop guide to preserve hands and feet. Cropping and focus are separate checks: a well-framed climber can still be out of focus.

Sources and scope

Apple: Use iPhone camera tools to set up your shot describes manual focus and exposure controls in its stated context. Apple: Use the Camera Control on iPhone documents its supported-model video focus/exposure method and the release-to-unlock limitation. These references do not establish identical controls on Android, every iPhone recording mode, or CruxCut.
