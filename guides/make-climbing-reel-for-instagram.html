How to Make a Climbing Reel: Crop, Trim, and Hook

Pick one attempt, open on the moment that matters, trim it, crop it tall without losing hands or feet, then watch the exported file before you post.

Published November 26, 2026 · By the CruxCut team

The short version

A good climbing reel is usually one attempt, trimmed to the part that matters, in a tall shape where the climber stays easy to see. This guide goes in the order most people edit: choose the clip, decide how it opens, trim, crop, add text, then export and check. The ideas about hooks and pacing are practical suggestions from our own editing experience, not guarantees about how any platform will show your video.

Step 1: Choose one attempt and one idea

Before you edit, decide what the reel is about. It might be a send, a fall at the crux, a new beta, or a funny moment. A reel that tries to show the whole session is hard to follow. If you filmed several tries, pick the one that makes the clearest point and leave the rest for another post. If you are not sure which attempt to use, our guide on reviewing climbing videos for beta can help you compare them, and organizing videos by problem and attempt makes the right clip easier to find.

Step 2: Decide how the reel opens

The first moments tell a viewer whether to keep watching, so it is worth choosing them on purpose. A useful starting point is to open on the moment you want people to care about: the start position, the hard move, or the fall. Cut the chalking up and the long walk back to the wall unless they are the joke. Then test it. Watch only the first two seconds and ask whether someone who does not know the route can tell what they are looking at. If the climber is a tiny figure in a wide gym shot, the opening will feel weak even when the climb is great, which is where the crop in step 4 helps.

Step 3: Trim to the part that matters

Trim the start so the reel opens where you decided in step 2, and trim the end soon after the outcome is clear. Keep enough of the finish or the fall that viewers see what happened. Trimming too tightly makes a clip feel abrupt, and trimming too loosely leaves dead time. The guide on where to trim a climbing video shows how to pick the start and end points. If you want to replay a hard move or change speed on purpose, do it deliberately and check the result, because an accidental speed change is a common surprise (see why a climbing video plays in slow motion).

On length, Instagram's Help Center says a reel can be up to 20 minutes and that reels over 3 minutes will not be recommended to new audiences (checked November 26, 2026). A single boulder attempt is far shorter, so length is rarely the limit. The real question is whether every second earns its place.

Step 4: Crop tall without losing hands or feet

Instagram's Help Center says you can upload a reel with an aspect ratio between 1.91:1 and 9:16, so 9:16 is the tallest shape accepted. Climbing is a tall activity, so a 9:16 crop usually fits it well, and it lets you zoom in so the climber fills more of the screen. The risk is the sides of the frame: an outstretched hand or foot can disappear when you crop narrower. Our guide on cropping vertically without cutting off hands or feet covers how to check this. If the climber moves up the wall, a single fixed crop may lose them, so read how to zoom in on a climber after filming from far away for the options, including a crop that follows the climber.

The same page says reels should have a minimum frame rate of 30 FPS and a minimum resolution of 720 pixels. Most current phone recordings meet that, but if you exported a very small copy or changed the frame rate, check the exported file.

Step 5: Add text and audio carefully

If you add a caption or a label such as the grade, keep it away from the climber's hands, feet, and the holds you want people to see. Text over the main action hides the thing viewers came for. Keep the wording short enough to read in a second or two. For sound, remember that audio and music rights are separate from your own footage, so use audio you are allowed to use on the platform. If the sound goes missing after editing, see why a climbing video has no sound after editing.

Step 6: Export, watch the saved file, then post

Export a new file and watch it from start to finish before you upload. A preview inside an editor and the exported file can look different, and a re-shared copy can look softer. If the posted version looks blurry, read why a climbing video looks blurry after sharing. If the file is too large to send, see how to make a smaller copy. If you are new to editing on your phone, the beginner steps for editing a bouldering video cover the basics in order.

Where a climbing-specific app fits

Checklist before you post

  • Is the reel about one attempt and one idea?
  • Do the first two seconds show what the viewer should care about?
  • Does the clip end soon after the outcome is clear?
  • Are hands and feet still visible in the tall crop?
  • Does any text stay away from the climber and the holds?
  • Did you watch the exported file, not just the preview?

Frequently asked questions

How do I make a climbing reel from a normal video?

Pick one attempt, trim it so it opens on the part you want people to see, crop it to a tall shape that keeps the climber's hands and feet in frame, and export a new file. Then watch the saved file before you post. Instagram's own Reels editor can also trim clips and add text, audio, and effects.

How long can a climbing reel be?

Instagram's Help Center says you can record or edit a reel up to 20 minutes long, and that reels over 3 minutes will not be recommended to new audiences (checked November 26, 2026). A single boulder attempt is usually far shorter than that, so length is rarely the limit. How much of the clip a viewer needs to see is the better guide.

What aspect ratio should a climbing reel be?

Instagram's Help Center says you can upload a reel with an aspect ratio between 1.91:1 and 9:16, so 9:16 is the tallest shape it accepts. Climbing is a vertical sport, so a tall shape often suits it, but check that hands and feet are not cut off at the sides.

What should the first seconds of a climbing reel show?

There is no rule that works for every clip. A useful starting point is to open on the moment you want viewers to care about, such as the start position, the crux, or the fall, rather than on someone chalking up. Test it by watching only the first two seconds and asking whether a stranger would know what they are looking at.

Do I need a special app to make a climbing reel?

No. Your phone's own tools and Instagram's editor can trim and add text. A climbing-specific app is mainly useful when you filmed from a tripod and want the crop to follow the climber, which is slow to do by hand. Review any automatic result before you post.
