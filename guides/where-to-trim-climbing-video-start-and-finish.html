Where to Trim a Climbing Video: Keep the Setup and Finish

Fix climbing clips that start mid-move or end too early. Choose readable trim points, preserve the outcome, and check the saved file before sharing.

Published November 2, 2026 · By the CruxCut team

Why a short clip can still feel incomplete

A climbing highlight can contain the hardest move and still be difficult to follow. If it begins after the feet leave the holds, the viewer misses the setup. If it stops on the first touch of the finish, the viewer may not know whether the climber stayed on. The fix is often a different start or end point, not more zoom or faster playback. This guide is about time boundaries, not crop shape or choosing an app.

First check whether the original contains the missing moment

Open the original recording before changing the short edit. Scrub backward from the featured move to find the preceding stable position. Then scrub forward past the final action to see what actually happened. If recording started late or stopped early, extending an edit cannot restore absent footage. Choose another attempt, use the available sequence honestly, or leave the outcome uncertain. Keep the original untouched so you can revise the cut later.

Pick the end before polishing the opening

Decide what this clip is meant to show: a controlled finish, an unsuccessful attempt, or a particular movement. For a successful attempt, keep the visible settling or control after the last move rather than cutting on contact alone. A video is not a substitute for the route or competition rules: do not claim an official completion based only on a brief touch. For an unsuccessful attempt, leave enough footage to understand the slip or release, without adding an unnecessarily long aftermath. Stop before unrelated activity takes over.

Move the start back to the last useful setup

From the featured move, step backward until the relevant hand position, foot placement, and movement direction make sense. Start before the weight shift or preparation you want the viewer to understand. Play from the proposed start without scrubbing: can a first-time viewer tell what the climber is trying to do? If not, move the start earlier. There is no universal number of lead-in seconds. A static setup may need little explanation; a coordinated jump or sequence can need more. Test readability rather than filling a fixed duration.

Use different boundaries for review and sharing

For beta review, keep the preceding move when it explains momentum, grip choice, or fatigue. A tighter sharing clip can omit unrelated waiting, but should not hide the action needed to understand the result. Save separate versions rather than turning your shortest edit into the only record. The beta-review guide covers comparison between attempts; the organization guide helps connect the short edit to its source.

Fix these three common cuts

  • The jump begins in mid-air. Move the start earlier until the takeoff and important foot setup are visible. If the source misses them, do not imply that they were recorded.
  • The clip ends at the first finish-hold touch. Extend it to show the observable result. If control remains unclear, avoid a caption that claims a send.
  • The clip includes a long walk to the camera. Trim that unrelated ending only after preserving the finish or fall context. Do not remove a later slip just to make an unsuccessful attempt look successful.

Check automatic suggestions against the full attempt

An automatically selected highlight is a starting point for review, not evidence that every important moment was included. Compare its first and last frames with the original and play the whole suggestion. If your editor supports boundary adjustment, extend the relevant edge. If it does not, keep a longer source segment or use another editor for that version. This is a general editing workflow, not a claim that every trim control is available in CruxCut. For the broader selection workflow, see automatic climbing highlights.

Make the final check in the saved file

Export and play the saved file from the beginning without touching the timeline. Confirm the setup is readable, the outcome remains visible, and no unexpected freeze or blank frame changes the meaning. Check the destination app's preview too: a cover image does not verify playback, and a loop can make an abrupt ending look different. If hands or feet disappear at the edge despite correct timing, that is a separate vertical-crop problem.

A quick boundary checklist

  • The original contains the setup and outcome I intend to show.
  • The clip starts before the meaningful preparation, not halfway through it.
  • The ending shows what happened, without claiming unseen control.
  • The short version does not replace my wider, longer review record.
  • I checked the exported file and have permission to share identifiable people.
