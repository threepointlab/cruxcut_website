Climbing video beta review

How to Review Climbing Videos for Better Beta

Use a full attempt to answer one specific movement question, compare visible differences, and decide what is worth testing next. Keep the review clip before making the reel.

Published October 29, 2026 · By the CruxCut team

Start with one question, not a full critique

A climbing video is easier to use when you know what you want to learn. Instead of asking why an attempt looked bad, pick one specific question: Did my foot leave the hold before I moved my hand? Did I pause before the next move? Was the intended foothold visible from this angle?

Write that question down before watching. You can revisit other details later. Trying to correct every movement at once makes it harder to know what changed on the next attempt.

Keep the complete attempt before making a highlight

Save the original recording. A short highlight can show the hardest move while removing the setup that explains it. For beta review, keep the approach to the crux, the move itself, and the moment immediately afterward. Include the start and finish when they matter to the question.

Use the wide source to check feet and surrounding holds. A close crop may be more engaging to share, but it can hide a foot adjustment or the position of the other hand. If the original does not show a contact, label it unknown rather than guessing. For recording basics, see how to film and edit climbing videos.

Watch once at normal speed, then inspect the sequence

Begin with a normal-speed replay so you can see the rhythm of the attempt. Then use pause or slow playback, where your player supports it, to inspect the short sequence around your question. Return to normal speed before deciding what to change: a slow replay can make a brief hesitation look much longer than it felt.

Check three moments: the position before the move, the first visible change, and the result. For example, a foot cutting loose after a hand slips is a different sequence from a foot leaving first. Video can help establish that order when the contacts are visible; it cannot prove which force or intention caused it.

Use a simple feet, hips, hands, timing checklist

  • Feet: Which foothold was used? Was there a visible adjustment? Could you see the foot during the move?
  • Hips: Where was the body relative to the holds? Note a visible change in position without treating a two-dimensional image as an exact distance measurement.
  • Hands: Which hand initiated the move, and what did the other hand do? Avoid assuming grip force from appearance alone.
  • Timing: Did the hands and feet move together or separately? Where did the climber pause?

These are prompts for observation, not a universal prescription. A useful position for one climber can be unsuitable for another because reach, mobility, preferred movement, and the intended sequence differ.

Compare two attempts at the same reference point

Pick two attempts on the same problem and, when possible, from the same camera position. Start both replays at a clear reference such as establishing the same foothold. Compare the same section, not the entire duration of one attempt against a highlight from another.

Two paused clips on separate screens are enough; your editor does not need a split-screen feature. Keep a short note for each attempt: the question, the visible difference, and what happened. Do not treat a send as proof that one particular detail caused success. Rest, familiarity, conditions, and several movement changes may all differ.

If you compare with another climber, ask what principle the sequence suggests rather than copying every body position. Also ask permission before saving or sharing someone else’s footage.

Choose one small, appropriate experiment for the next attempt

Turn your observation into a single question you can test, such as whether using the already identified foothold earlier makes the sequence easier to organize. Keep the test within your normal ability and the gym’s rules. A clip is not a reason to take an unsafe fall, ignore discomfort, or change a belayer’s position.

After the next attempt, check whether the intended change actually happened before judging its effect. If the camera angle hides the important contact, improve the recording rather than assigning a confident explanation to missing information.

Make the beta clip and the sharing clip separately

Keep one version with enough context for review. For a social edit, you may choose a shorter sequence or a tighter frame, but check that the movement still reads clearly. Keep the original so you can return to the full attempt later.

If a moving crop follows the wrong person or hides the feet, compare it with the source and read our tracking troubleshooting guide. If your goal is a short recap after the review, see our climbing highlights workflow.

A quick review note you can reuse

  • Problem or route and recording date
  • One question for this replay
  • What is clearly visible, and what is hidden?
  • One difference between attempts
  • One appropriate thing to try or ask a coach about

The goal is a more useful next question, not a perfect diagnosis from one camera angle. Video does not replace safe climbing practice, a qualified coach, or medical advice about pain or injury.
